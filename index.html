The model

Make responsibilities, sequence, support, and readiness visible.

The work is hard, and effort does not make it less hard. The one lever on that half is sequence. The other half, fragmented information, unwritten priorities, and a year with no shape, we built. We can take it back out.

Transition and onboarding are the same season. Onboarding falls apart when we treat it as a July event. It starts in March and runs through July. Whichever season breaks you most is the one you start with.

The four-part sustainable system

When all four are visible, a new administrator can see the whole job and their place in it.

R

Responsibilities

Every recurring function has a named trainer, and that person knows it.

S

Sequence

Work is mapped to the season it belongs in, so nothing arrives as a surprise.

S

Support

An assigned peer, a written coverage plan, and protected learning time.

R

Readiness

An observable finish line tells you someone can run a function alone.

The day-one package

Four things that exist before the person walks in.

Modules and acronyms

The accreditation language everyone else in the room already speaks. Week one.

Key contacts, by question

Not only names, but who to go to for which question, so the first instinct is not a guess. Week one.

Academic year calendar

One view of every deadline, meeting, and recurring responsibility. Week one.

An assigned buddy

A named, experienced colleague, matched deliberately rather than offered informally. Day one.

If your institution does nothing else after the session, do these four. They take one afternoon to build, and you only build them once. The checklist is in the worksheets.

The first-year arc

Cognitive load changes across the first year. Support should change with it.

First 30 days

Decode the landscape

New language, systems, people, and deadlines compete for limited working memory. Orientation load.

Days 31 to 90

Perform with guidance

Real tasks while still checking sequence, ownership, and risk. Dual task load.

Months 4 to 6

Connect the system

Separate tasks become patterns across accreditation, recruitment, evaluations, and operations. Integration load.

Months 7 to 12

Carry the cycle

More work owned independently, including recurring deadlines not yet seen. Ownership load.

Early on, orient. Then guide practice. Next, help people connect patterns. Finally, support independent ownership without disappearing. The stages are the presenters' applied framework, not stages defined in Sweller's work.

Support roles

One person should not hold every support role.

Trainer

Teaches a specific function and reviews the work.

Buddy

Answers everyday questions, explains local practices, and offers a lower-stakes place to ask.

Manager or program leader

Clarifies expectations, sets priorities, gives feedback, and addresses workload and barriers.

Mentor

Supports broader development and a longer-term professional identity.

Handoff

Look at what changes hands.

What we usually hand over

  • System access
  • An inherited task list
  • An open door

Useful, but incomplete.

What the role requires

  • A written coverage plan
  • Sequenced training
  • A named trainer for every function

A framework and a person.

Structured handoffs are expected on the clinical side, planned ahead, with named people and continuity. Bring that same discipline to our own handoffs. This is a design choice, not an accreditation requirement.

Scaling

Adapt the model to the size of your program.

Smaller program

  • Combine functions into broader training categories
  • Use shared institutional experts for specialized work
  • Assign a buddy outside the immediate program when necessary

Larger program or Sponsoring Institution

  • Standardize common functions centrally
  • Retain program-specific training locally
  • Use shared templates and common readiness indicators
  • Maintain a trainer roster by function

  1. Ewen AM, et al. Program Administrator Burnout in Graduate Medical Education: A Longitudinal Study. J Gen Intern Med. 2020;35:3248-3253. Personal and work-related burnout highest at the July baseline, lowest in March; greater isolation associated with higher burnout.
  2. Ewen AM, et al. Program administrator burnout, national survey. J Grad Med Educ. 2019. n = 931. Mean personal 53.7, work-related 52.0, client-related 30.6 (Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, 0 to 100). Administrators who had considered leaving had higher scores in all three domains.
  3. Feist et al. J Grad Med Educ. 2019. Cited in the session for role scope and structural contributors.
  4. Eckart et al. J Grad Med Educ. 2019. 72% of program administrators spent more than 10% of their time on resident well-being.
  5. ACGME national survey of GME program and institutional coordinators. Conducted late 2022; results published in Academic Medicine and reported by ACGME, June 2025. 24% depressive symptoms, 30% moderate to severe anxiety, 38% burnout, 28% likely to leave within a year.
  6. Weindorf. Six no-cost events; isolation fell and well-being rose. Cited in the session.
  7. World Health Organization. Burn-out an "occupational phenomenon." ICD-11, 2019. Three dimensions: exhaustion, mental distance, reduced efficacy.
  8. Sweller J. Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. Cognitive Science. 1988;12(2):257-285. The first-year stages on this site are the presenters' applied framework, not stages from Sweller's work.
  9. ACGME Program Coordinator Handbook, 2024. Role scope: accreditation management, trainee well-being, data reporting, operational leadership, committee governance.
  10. Phoenix Children's Hospital Training Plan and Checklist for New Program Administrators, 2026. Source of the 35+ recurring-function list and the training plan structure shown in the session.
